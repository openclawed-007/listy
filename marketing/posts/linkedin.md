## Post

```
I spent a weekend building a shopping list app. Then I spent three months learning why every shopping list app fails.

It's not the list. Lists are easy.

It's the moment you say to the other people in your house: "Download this and make an account."

That's where it dies. The list ends up on one phone, and the person in the shop is texting "did you say oat or almond?"

So I changed the question. Instead of "how do I get everyone to install this", I asked "what's the least someone could do and still help?"

The answer turned out to be: scan a QR code on the fridge.

That's it. No download. No sign-up. They're looking at the live list on their own phone, ticking things off, adding things. The person who owns the list stays in control — guests can never delete anything, and that's enforced on the server, not just hidden in the interface.

A few things I took away from building it:

→ The hardest onboarding problem is usually not yours. It's the second user's. Design for them.

→ "What it doesn't do" is a feature. No ads, no data selling, no account for helpers. People trust the negatives more than the positives.

→ Boring products need one sharp idea. The list itself is deliberately dull. The fridge QR is the whole bet.

It's free and open source. Link in the comments if you want to try it — or if you just want to argue about whether oat milk belongs in "Dairy".
```
